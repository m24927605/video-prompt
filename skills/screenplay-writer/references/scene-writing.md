# Scene and sequence writing

Read this before drafting or continuing screenplay pages.

## Load only what can change the scene

For a bounded scene, load:

- its approved scene card or the user's direct brief;
- the preceding and following scene when they exist;
- involved characters' current goals, relationships, knowledge, injuries, wardrobe, and possessions;
- current truth, audience knowledge, open setups/payoffs, and world rules that affect the scene;
- locked lines or events;
- the selected project-voice axes.

Exclude unrelated lore and distant backstory. Keep any fact whose absence would make the scene wrong.

## Scene contract

Before writing, state internally:

1. **Entrance state** — what each character expects and what pressure already exists.
2. **Immediate objective** — what each person tries to obtain, prevent, conceal, test, or force now.
3. **Resistance** — an active person, system, physical condition, time limit, conflicting duty, or internal refusal expressed through behavior.
4. **Information asymmetry** — truth, audience knowledge, and each character's knowledge are not interchangeable.
5. **Tactics** — characters must try methods, notice results, and adapt.
6. **Turn** — leverage, interpretation, relationship, risk, commitment, or the practical situation changes. A quiet scene may intentionally hold, but the reason and value of that stasis must be clear.
7. **Exit delta** — identify what is different and what obligation now passes forward.

Do not render these labels into the screenplay. They are hidden operating state.

## Choose the scene engine

A scene is not automatically an argument. Select the mechanism that best pressures this story:

- negotiation, test, concealment, seduction, bargaining, interrogation, evasion;
- task under pressure, rescue, repair, performance, ritual, game, transaction;
- discovery, verification, surveillance, mistaken interpretation, delayed recognition;
- reunion, aftermath, care, waiting, grief, celebration, humiliation;
- pursuit, escape, infiltration, containment, countdown, collision;
- public mask versus private need;
- two people cooperating toward incompatible ends.

Adjacent scenes should vary engines when possible. Three consecutive scenes of polite conversation that each end in a small reveal remain one repeated scene shape.

## Entrance, tactics, turn, exit

- Enter after the obvious setup whenever the missing setup is inferable.
- Give the first image or action an immediate job: pressure, relationship, contradiction, information, or tone.
- Let tactics fail for character-specific reasons. A failure caused by generic interruption or sudden stupidity is usually writer convenience.
- Make the turn visible in behavior: someone sits, leaves, hands over an object, changes the subject, tells a different lie, stops helping, accepts a cost, or realizes the other person knows.
- Exit before interpretation. The final image, action, sound, refusal, or line should create an after-state without a summary paragraph.

## Filmable action

Action lines primarily describe what an audience can see or hear and what performers can play.

- Prefer active present-tense verbs and concrete spatial relations.
- Translate load-bearing interiority into choice, blocking, timing, object handling, speech, silence, mistakes, or an authorized voice-over.
- A brief subjective phrase is allowed when it changes how the moment should read; sustained interior explanation belongs in prose, not action.
- Describe effects rather than directing every shot. Use a camera instruction only when the specific reveal, point of view, or visual grammar cannot be communicated otherwise.
- Make entrances, exits, eyelines, doors, distances, handoffs, and object ownership understandable enough to stage.
- Let production design carry history through specific, relevant wear and use; do not decorate every surface.
- Sounds can initiate action, contradict the image, bridge scenes, expose off-screen space, or become a character's tactic.

Action paragraph length serves reading speed. Keep ordinary blocks short, but allow a longer paragraph when geography or sustained action genuinely requires it. Do not create a mechanical pattern of one-line blocks.

## Quiet scenes

A scene does not need a twist, revelation, confrontation, or hook merely to prove it is dramatic. A quiet or texture scene earns its place when it changes at least one of:

- intimacy or distance;
- what a character will do next;
- what the audience notices or suspects;
- the cost of an earlier event;
- the meaning of a repeated action, object, place, or silence;
- the tempo required before or after a high-pressure sequence.

Do not add crying, confession, shouting, surprise visitors, accidents, or thematic lines to rescue a deliberately quiet scene.

## Action sequences

- Establish the physical objective, geography, hazards, and changing constraints before multiplying moves.
- Track cause, contact, momentum, damage, resources, and fatigue.
- Let action reveal decision-making and relationships; choreography without character consequence is coverage, not drama.
- Vary scale and tempo. Continuous escalation at one register becomes flat.
- Preserve continuity of injury, wardrobe, carried objects, exits, vehicles, and elapsed time.

## Sequence integration

Draft adjacent scenes serially around a sequence question. After each promoted scene:

1. record its exit delta;
2. confirm the next scene begins from that state;
3. compare its engine and rhythm with the previous two scenes;
4. check whether the sequence pressure changed in kind or only volume;
5. update continuity before drafting forward.

Different writers may generate variants of one scene, but one lead writer must integrate the canonical sequence. Never concatenate independently written adjacent scenes without a voice and continuity pass.

## Scene completion check

- The scene honors every locked event, line, fact, and stop point.
- Character actions follow available knowledge and current relationships.
- Tactics change in response to resistance.
- The audience can understand essential geography without a shot list.
- Exposition appears only where a character has a reason to control, conceal, contest, or act on it.
- Meaning is carried by events, images, sound, and performance space rather than author explanation.
- The exit delta and next obligation are explicit outside the screenplay and implicit inside it.
