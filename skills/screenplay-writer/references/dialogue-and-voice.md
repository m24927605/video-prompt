# Dialogue, character voice, and performance

Read this for dialogue-heavy scenes, character voice design, dialogue polish, or a table-read pass.

## Voice card

Build a compact voice card for every major speaking character. A voice is a behavior under social pressure, not a catchphrase.

When a bounded request lacks prior voice material, create only a provisional, scene-local card from facts in the request and the characters' immediate tactics. Mark biography, dialect, and cross-scene habits unknown rather than inventing them; do not claim cross-scene voice continuity until evidence exists.

| Axis | Record |
|---|---|
| Language source | region, generation, class, education, work, family, community, and languages actually spoken |
| Sentence shape | fragments or complete thoughts, short or recursive syntax, interruption pattern, speed, pauses |
| Default tactic | direct, charm, lecture, tease, appease, intimidate, evade, bargain, test, perform, withdraw |
| Information behavior | what they volunteer, hide, distort, assume, verify, or refuse to name |
| Relationship register | names, titles, pronouns, honorifics, politeness, and vocabulary used with each person |
| Humor and aggression | temperature, targets, timing, profanity, taboo, self-protection |
| Pressure states | how speech changes when safe, challenged, cornered, ashamed, or out of control |
| Silence behavior | whether they fill silence, weaponize it, flee it, misread it, or answer through action |
| Contradiction | gap between what they say, want, fear, believe, and do |

Use culturally and linguistically plausible choices. Do not create accent by misspelling every word or reducing a community to stock slang.

## Dialogue engine

Before each exchange, define for every participant:

- immediate agenda;
- leverage and vulnerability;
- known facts, suspected facts, and false beliefs;
- public mask and private cost;
- current tactic and the condition that will make it change;
- what cannot be said safely in this setting.

Dialogue becomes dramatic when agendas collide and tactics adapt. It becomes mechanical when each line simply answers the previous question or transfers information.

## Write tactics, not meanings

Characters rarely state the screenplay's diagnosis of themselves.

- A request may be disguised as advice, a joke, a complaint, a correction, or a practical task.
- A refusal may arrive as delay, over-compliance, a change of subject, a question, or attention to an object.
- A confession may be incomplete, badly timed, self-serving, or understood differently by the listener.
- A threat can be polite; affection can be rude; expertise can sound uncertain when status is at risk.
- A line's literal content and dramatic action should not always match.

Subtext must come from interests, history, power, and risk. Deliberately vague dialogue without a concrete pressure underneath is not subtext.

## The unspoken line

For a beat whose weight sits in silence, in a held look, or in a line that means the opposite of what it says, write the character's unspoken sentence — what they are saying to themselves at that moment — and keep it off the page.

- It is hidden operating state, like the scene contract. It never becomes dialogue, a parenthetical, a voice-over, or an action line that reports the feeling.
- Use it as a test, not as content. The visible behavior written on the page must be producible by a performer playing that sentence. If the same action would equally serve three unrelated unspoken lines, the beat is under-written. If it only reads to someone who has been told the sentence, it is over-interior, and the beat needs a choice, an object, or a tactic instead.
- Keep it in the character's own register and inside their knowledge. A sentence more articulate than the character could think is the writer's diagnosis of the scene, and the pages will drift toward explaining it.
- One line per participant per beat is usually enough. A running interior track is prose, and it will not survive contact with a performer.
- Where a scene is deliberately silent, the unspoken lines are also the record of who is refusing to say what, and why the refusal costs something.

## Tactic shifts

Mark the exchange by tactic changes rather than equal turns:

```text
probe → joke → deny → offer → accuse → withdraw
```

Do not force a fixed number of shifts. A single line can reverse leverage; a long exchange may circle because neither character can afford the next move. The shift must respond to something observable in the scene.

## Exposition

- Never let characters explain facts all participants already know for the audience's benefit.
- Deliver background through disagreement, correction, incomplete recall, work, evidence, consequences, or a newcomer who has a legitimate need to know.
- Give information an owner. Different characters frame the same fact according to interest and knowledge.
- Split explanation across action and time when the audience does not need it all now.
- Technical or procedural dialogue must affect a decision, risk, relationship, or immediate task.
- If a fact can be understood from action or production design, remove the explanatory line.

## Silence, interruption, and action

An unanswered line is still a dialogue beat.

- Use silence when answering would cost more than not answering.
- Interruptions may be intentional, accidental, physical, or self-censorship; do not add them as random humanizing noise.
- Let characters mishear, answer the safer question, continue a task, leave, or touch the wrong object.
- Parentheticals should clarify an otherwise ambiguous target or action, not prescribe every emotion to the actor.
- Do not pair every line with a gesture. Place action where it changes leverage, reveals contradiction, or gives the exchange breath.

## Distinguish voices without caricature

Run the hidden-cue test: remove character cues from a representative exchange. Major lines should remain attributable through agenda, information access, syntax, relationship language, and tactics—not only a repeated verbal tic.

If voices blur, change one or two load-bearing axes per character. Do not maximize every difference; people from the same family, workplace, or region should share some language.

Check the same character across relationships. Consistency means the same underlying person adapts, not that they speak with one immutable surface style.

## Dialogue polish operation

When the user asks only for dialogue polish:

1. lock scene order, events, outcomes, character decisions, knowledge, and action outside the exchange;
2. diagnose interchangeable lines, exposition, missing tactics, tone/register drift, and unplayable phrasing;
3. revise only dialogue and the minimum adjacent action needed for timing or clarity;
4. report any structural defect that dialogue alone cannot solve without silently fixing it;
5. preserve locked lines verbatim.

## Table-read gate

Read every line aloud mentally or with performers when available:

- Can the actor pursue something with the line?
- Does it fit the character's mouth, setting, era, relationship, and current pressure?
- Is the thought too complete, balanced, or polished for the moment?
- Does the line repeat what the action already showed?
- Does the response acknowledge, deflect, exploit, or misunderstand the previous move?
- Would a silence or action create more playable pressure?
- Where the beat turns on what is not said, can a performer play the unspoken line through what is written?
- Are joke, dialect, profanity, and formality earned by this character rather than spread evenly across the cast?

Do not make every line maximally clever, quotable, elliptical, or distinct. Ordinary connective speech is necessary contrast; polish only the lines that need dramatic precision.
